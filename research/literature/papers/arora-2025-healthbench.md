## Question
How can open-ended answers in an expert domain be scored at scale so that the score is close to
what experts would give?

## Data
5,000 multi-turn health conversations; rubrics written by 262 physicians, 48,562 unique criteria
(median 11 per conversation, range 2 to 48).

## Method
- Each criterion is a self-contained statement with a point value from -10 to +10; negative
  criteria describe errors or harmful content. A model grader (GPT-4.1) decides each criterion
  independently, met or not met. Example score = points earned over maximum positive points;
  overall score = mean, clipped to [0, 1].
- Meta-evaluation on 34 consensus criteria (assigned to a conversation only when most reviewing
  physicians agree it applies): over 60,000 physician met/not-met labels; macro F1 of the grader
  against physicians, compared with each physician against the others.
- Checks for length effects (correlation of length and score; length-controlled win rates),
  run-to-run variation over 16 runs, and worst-of-k reliability.

## Outcome variable and horizon
Rubric score; grader-physician macro F1. Not a forecasting task.

## Result
Empirical findings.

- **Criterion-level grading by a model matches the typical physician.** GPT-4.1's macro F1 beats
  the average physician in five of seven themes and sits above the 33rd physician percentile in
  all seven (for example context seeking 0.706 against 0.646; health data tasks 0.683 against
  0.730). Physician-physician and model-physician agreement both lie between 55% and 75%.
- **Grader size matters.** Macro F1: GPT-4.1 0.709, o4-mini 0.692, o3 0.681, GPT-4.1 mini 0.661,
  GPT-4.1 nano 0.580.
- **Length bias is small but present.** Length-score correlation r = -0.053 (GPT-4o) and 0.123
  (o3); one head-to-head comparison reverses under length control.
- **Scores are stable run to run** (about 0.002 standard deviation over 16 runs); worst-of-16
  falls about a third below the mean for the best model.
- **Scores spread widely:** about 16% (GPT-3.5 Turbo), 32% (GPT-4o), 60% (o3).

## Look-ahead and memorisation
Not addressed beyond keeping the rubrics private to the benchmark at creation.

## What it settles for us
- **The rubric design to copy for open-ended S1 analysis: many small, binary, item-specific
  criteria with signed points, graded one at a time.** For an earnings release, positive criteria
  can be generated from the gold figures ("states that operating margin fell") and negative
  criteria from contradictions ("states that revenue grew" when it fell; cites a figure off by
  more than the tolerance). Generated by rule, they need no physician-style authors (our design
  inference).
- **Validate the grader with a meta-evaluation set and compare it with human-human agreement**,
  not with an assumed perfect human. The ceiling is low: experts agree 55% to 75% on criteria
  that need interpretation. Rule-generated factual criteria should do better; that is
  measurable.
- **Small graders lose agreement** (0.58 for the nano model against 0.71). A 0.8B or 4B model is
  not a candidate grader; nor is any roster model, under rule 6.
- **Report worst-of-k as well as the mean** when comparing a student and its teacher; distilled
  models may differ more in reliability than in mean (hypothesis).

## Limits
- Meta-evaluation covers only the 34 consensus criteria, not the conversation-specific ones that
  make up most of the score.
- The grader prompt was tuned with GPT-4.1, which may favour it, as the authors note.
- Physician baselines were weak and short; the task framing was unusual for them.
- Health advice, not financial analysis.
